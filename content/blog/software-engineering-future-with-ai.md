Whenever I'm asking the same question "How do you see the future of Software engineering?" from the people I know and whose seniority I respect, mostly I get a short, but smart, answer from them: "I don't know".

Things keep changing… and change is the most frustrating feeling! While you're learning new skills, newer skills are appearing, even worse, what you're learning might get some kind of out of date! Can you believe it? But then what?

In this article I'm going to write my experience as I'm continuing my journey with Artificial Intelligence! Our beloved friend, colleague or competitor!

I'll start with some of my predictions, right or wrong:

Right ones:

- Frontend guys who aren't "Engineer" but are more of a "Code monkey" will have a hard time finding a new job.
- You can't afford to only be an expert in a library / framework (React developer / PHP developer). You must know more about what's happening around you. If you're one of those who might reply with: "But I just got a job as a PHP developer," my answer is: Yes you did! But look how many new vacancies require you to be a Software engineer AND an expert in one or more fields, you can't just fit in by being an expert in one field while missing the required software engineering skills.
- Soon the software engineering job market will go down and there will be too many layoffs. This job market situation has a second part which we should see whether it's going to happen or not: but this deflation is temporary and the job market will show more demand for engineers, but this time for a different type of engineer.

Wrong ones:

- CLI will be the future!
- Frontend roles are harder to be replaced by the AI
- It'll take years for companies to adopt these new changes.

This article is like an experiment: I'm going to write about my prediction of the future, then propose a way for software engineers / developers, and later on we can assess how far off I've predicted it, it's a learning for all of us!

I'll start with a bit of a context. We're living in a world that you won't even write an article without proofreading it using the AI.

In this world, the usage of the AI, whether you like it or not, is there! IS is a disruptive technology.

You can't go back in time when there was no AI.

Think about the time when we were scared of losing our files. Now you have a backup somewhere on the cloud, you probably easily hit the "delete" button on your keyboard. It wasn't always like this! It took our folks some time to convince people to trust "Cloud".

But let's see how AI needed people's buy-in to accept it as something new: "So you can just type and talk to ChatGPT and ask anything!" "What?? How's this even free?" If you don't remember, this is how AI days started!

People are loving it whilst they're aware it's not accurate! It wasn't long before you saw people, even including experts, start to become "prompt engineers" and show how useful it could be!

Everybody was super excited about it. It was actually hard to ask people not to use it!

However, some companies, for the IP (Intellectual Property) restriction reasons, started to restrict the usage but even that didn't last long!

After all, OpenAI, Anthropic, and other AI giants had convinced the legal teams that "This is safe if you just pay us waaay more!"

In this article I only aim to cover how AI has impacted software engineering roles! It's so hard not to see how it has already changed people's ways of working.

As an individual contributor, these days, you aren't just trying to write your program all by yourself, even if you're doing it you know that AI could help you fairly accurately autocomplete some lines of your code, not to mention writing whole functions far more effectively: writing better doc strings, refactoring bits you forgot to handle, troubleshooting the bug quicker, etc.

It's very hard to refuse to see all these changes, unless you're intentionally turning your head away. Don't worry if you've done so, so did I for a while!

It's quite understandable not to trust such uncertain results especially if you're working on some parts of the system where an error isn't super cheap to fix.

But I came to believe it's not just hype, and it's here to stay.

So now instead of trying to find evidence that proves me right: "Look AI failed here! Yesss, we aren't losing our jobs to it", I'm looking to figure out what I'm supposed to do now.

Whenever I get lost, I start from the beginning. I did the same this time.

I started by asking "Who is a software engineer?", then followed by "Why software engineering is needed?" You could continue these with similar and still fundamental questions starting with "What, and How, …".

I'm not going to answer all these questions in this very article, but I'll write more about it in my blog. I'll conclude with some of the lessons I had along the way:

- Don't think you missed a whole new world: It's always like you're missing out on a huge change, but you realise most of it is gone and important bits are staying. It's not to discourage curiosity! It's to be more comfortable to learn new things!
- Basics won't change. If you still don't have a clear idea about how a computer works, it's more important than ever to go and learn. You don't need to stop everything and just go learn the basics, then start again. No, you can start to dig a bit deeper. I promise you can quickly learn the basics. Next time if you're unsure about whether to use a Sync or an Async function in your JS program. Think more about the "why?" in your judgment.
- Don't skip learning because it's soon going to be outdated. I see value in reading about what's new in the field and how people are changing their way of day-to-day working. If this is going to be the stable one, which I don't believe is coming any time soon, you'll win! You're early in the new world, and if not you have a history that helps you better follow up the next "why?"s.
